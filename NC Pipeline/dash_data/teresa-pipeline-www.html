The idea

Distance alone doesn't tell you if a store works for someone. It might be too expensive, closed when they get off work, or have empty shelves. These are hard to measure with surveys, but people already write about them in Google reviews. Teresa's method treats those reviews as a huge, accidental survey.

The five dimensions of access

From Penchansky and Thomas (1981), adapted to food. This is what every review is eventually sorted into.

DimensionPlain meaningA review might say
AvailabilityIs there enough food, and is it stocked?"Lots of empty shelves. Did not find what I need."
AccessibilityCan I physically get there?"Easy to get to, easy to get in and out of."
AccommodationDoes it fit my life? Hours, payment, SNAP or WIC"Love that it's open to 12am, I work nights."
AffordabilityPrices and value for money"Great deals." or "Way overpriced."
AcceptabilityDoes it meet my standards? Freshness, cleanliness, staff"Disgusting, expired milk on the shelf."

Why it's worth doing: in Raleigh, two Harris Teeters with the same 4.4★ rating had very different profiles. Star ratings hide this. Once Teresa required at least average Affordability and Acceptability, the nearest acceptable store moved from 0.63 to 1.93 miles away.

    Why embeddings work, and what they miss

    To test the model, we gave it five short sentences and asked how close each pair is in meaning. Three are about prices, one is about a cashier and one is about parking.

    How to read the table

    1. Find one sentence on the left and another along the top. The number where they cross is how close they are in meaning.
    2. Near 1 means almost the same meaning. Near 0 means unrelated. Darker cells are closer.
    3. "Same" marks a sentence compared with itself. The top half is blank because it would only repeat the bottom half.

    What it shows: "outrageous" and "great deals" are opposite opinions, yet they score 0.55. That is almost as close as two complaints about prices (0.61), and far above anything paired with the cashier or parking (0.21 at most). The model groups reviews by what they are about, not by whether the writer is happy.

    Why it matters: one topic can hold both praise and complaints. So the text can tell us the dimension, such as Affordability, but not good or bad. That is why stage 8 uses stars for direction.

    Five sentences are an illustration, not a measurement. The scores are cosine similarity from all-MiniLM-L12-v2, the model used in stage 3.

    How we chose 83 topics

    Teresa's minimum cluster size of 120 suited a corpus of about 33,000 reviews. On 975,388 reviews, even a size of 250 gives 582 topics, too many for a person to check. We tried six sizes. We chose 2,000: coherence equals Raleigh, diversity is the best of the six, and 83 topics is still reviewable.

    Dashed line: the Raleigh model. Coherence rises as topics get broader, so it can't decide this alone. NC coherence uses a 100,000-review sample, so treat the match with Raleigh as close, not exact.

    Stars already separate complaints from praise

    No star ratings went into the clustering, yet topics about rude staff, hours and pickup orders average far below the overall ★. That is a good sign that the topics mean something.
